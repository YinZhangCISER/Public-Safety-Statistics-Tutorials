# Topic 7: What Makes a Good Comparison Group

> **Developed by Yin Zhang, PhD**, Assistant Professor, Department of Mathematics and Statistics, Washington State University

**The question this module answers:** *Which agencies belong in the comparison group, and does picking similar ones matter more than picking several?*

---

## The Core Concept

The instinct is to find the agency that looks most like yours: same size, same region, same kind of community. That instinct is half right.

**What a comparison group has to do is move the way your agency would have moved.** Looking alike helps with that, but two things matter more:

1. **It must not have been affected by the program.**
2. **It must be big enough, or numerous enough, to have a steady line.**

A single very similar agency is usually a worse comparison than seven mixed ones, because one agency's line jumps around for reasons that have nothing to do with anything.

## Why It Matters

Peer comparison in public safety is often done by picking the one or two agencies a chief considers most comparable. That produces a comparison whose year to year movement is mostly accident, and it invites an accusation that is hard to answer: that the peers were chosen to produce the answer.

Choosing **all** the agencies that meet a stated rule, before looking at the outcome, avoids both problems at once.

## The Example

Two candidate comparison groups for the same trained agencies.

![Two panels side by side, each showing the trained agencies as a blue line. In the left panel the comparison is all seven untrained agencies, drawn as a smooth green line running roughly parallel below the blue. In the right panel the comparison is Orrindale alone, drawn as an orange line that swings up and down across a wide range](Figures/fig_07_good_comparison.png)

The left panel uses all seven agencies that never took the training. Their combined line is smooth, and it runs roughly parallel to the trained agencies. Whatever gap opens after the program is readable.

The right panel uses **Orrindale** alone, which has eight sworn officers and averages under one use of force incident a month. Its line swings across a range wider than any plausible program effect. Compared against this, the trained agencies would look dramatically better in some years and dramatically worse in others, depending only on which year you chose to look.

**Orrindale is not a bad agency to compare against because it is unlike the others.** It is bad because one small agency's numbers move so much on their own that they cannot detect anything.

## What To Watch For

- **Set the rule before you look at the outcome.** "All agencies in the region that did not take the program" is a rule. "The agencies that make the numbers look sensible" is not, and a reader will notice.
- **Pool rather than pick.** Seven agencies averaged together give a much steadier line than the best single choice.
- **Exclude agencies that took a similar program.** Under any name.
- **Similar is about movement, not appearance.** An agency of a different size that moves in step is a better comparison than a twin that does not. [Topic 9](Topic_09_Were_They_Moving_Together_Before.md) is the test.
- **Say who is in and who is out, and why.** Every exclusion is a decision that affects the answer.

## 💡 The Insight

A comparison group's job is to be steady and unaffected, not to look like you.

## Check Your Understanding

<details>
<summary><b>1.</b> A chief proposes comparing against "the agency across the county line that is most like us." What are the two objections?</summary>

First, one agency's line moves for its own reasons, so the comparison will be noisy no matter how similar the agency is. Second, the agency across the county line is the most likely place for the program to have spilled over: shared training academies, officers who move between the two, and mutual aid on the same calls. Being similar and adjacent makes it a worse comparison on the second count, not a better one. See [Topic 10](Topic_10_When_The_Comparison_Group_Moves_Too.md).
</details>

<details>
<summary><b>2.</b> Is it acceptable to drop an agency from the comparison group after seeing the results?</summary>

Only if you say so and give a reason that does not refer to the results. "We removed Havenbrook because it changed how it classifies calls in January 2023" is a reason. "We removed Havenbrook because it made the effect smaller" is not. Report the estimate both ways when you drop anything, so a reader can see what the decision cost.
</details>

<details>
<summary><b>3.</b> The trained agencies and the comparison agencies start at clearly different levels. Should the comparison group be reweighted to match?</summary>

Not at this level of analysis, and usually not at any. A difference in level cancels out of the comparison. Reweighting to match levels is a real technique, but it introduces its own assumptions and it is not needed to fix a problem that subtraction already handles. What cannot be fixed by subtraction is a difference in direction, which is the next topic.
</details>

## Key Takeaway

Write down the rule for who is in the comparison group before you see any results, then include everyone the rule admits.
